    What makes it compact

    • The TDS probe lies across the chamber instead of standing in it, right under the flow sensor’s outlet. The chamber dropped from 45.5 to 25.4 mm tall, and the jet now hits the probe, so every drop passes the needles.
    • All the electronics stand around the flow sensor in the space beside its turbine drum: ESP32 north, TDS board south, battery west, perfboard east.
    • No aluminium tube. The printed shell is thinner, lets the radio through and has a hex socket that grips the adapter.
    • The charger sits beside the chamber with its USB-C port facing down, so charging adds no height and water can’t run into the port.
    • The report’s Ø60 × 85 mm is still out of reach with these parts: the YF-S201 alone is 63.5 mm long, and the 42 × 32 mm TDS board needs about 61 mm of width beside its 35 mm drum.

    Buy these too (small parts)

    • LiPo 803450, about 1500 mAh, with a protection board.
    • Chrome M22 × G½ adapter (hex 27 mm), a ½" rubber washer, a 45 × 1.5 mm nitrile O-ring.
    • 3 fused-quartz discs 6 × 1 mm, a small UV-C boost driver.
    • 3 × M2.5 × 12 self-tapping screws, 2 × AO3401 MOSFETs, a 9 mm passive buzzer.
    • Food-safe silicone, 2 mm double-sided foam tape, 0.2 mm enamelled wire for the piezo.

    Build notes

    • Measure first: TDS probe head, Neoperl insert, battery, DevKit and the adapter hex. Change the numbers at the top of make_printed_parts.py and run it again.
    • Pull the plastic strips off the DevKit’s pin headers and snip the pins; pull the two white plug housings off the TDS board.
    • Upload the firmware before assembly. Later updates go over Wi-Fi.
    • For taps up to about 50 °C. The TDS probe is rated 55 °C and the battery sits next to the sensor.
